01 Overview

Protocols tell us what to do.
But is that enough?

Laboratory work is complex, precise, and sensitive to error. Yet the protocols that guide it often leave experimenters searching for details, interpreting ambiguous instructions, and interrupting their work.

This research explores how augmented reality could better support these workflows. Through semi-structured interviews and participatory design with a lightweight AR prototype, we examine experimenters’ practices, information needs, and expectations for future procedural guidance.

Our contribution is a human-centered understanding of the design space: how laboratory guidance can become clearer, multimodal, adaptive, and accessible.

Read the full abstract +

Laboratory protocols serve as essential procedural guidance tools that support consistent outcomes and scientific innovation, yet they often fall short of facilitating reproducible results in real-world applications. Current methods for enhancing reproducibility in routine task protocols do not readily translate to laboratory experiments, due to their high procedural complexity and low tolerance for error. Inspired by the potential of Augmented Reality (AR) for procedural guidance, this paper explores the design of AR-based tools to support reproducible laboratory workflows. We conducted semi-structured interviews and participatory design sessions, using a lightweight AR prototype as a probe, to examine current protocol practices and challenges, and uncover experimenters’ procedural information needs. Our findings reveal experimenters’ expectations for the role of AR in experimental workflows and their concerns about its use. Based on these insights, we identify design opportunities for AR-based procedural guidance tools that better align with experimenters’ needs and expectations, with the potential to enhance experimental efficiency.

The AR design probe

A shared view of
text and demonstration.

Built in Unity and deployed on HoloLens 2, the lightweight prototype places video tutorials and textual protocols on configurable virtual panels. Spatial anchoring and voice commands support access during hands-on work.

The prototype was a research probe for eliciting design insights. The study did not quantitatively evaluate improvements in reproducibility or task performance.

03 Key findings

What should the next
generation of protocols offer?

Four connected directions emerged from experimenters’ reflections on the AR probe.

Finding 01 / Information structure

More precise.
Better organized.

Experimenters wanted explicit operational details, reliable guidance for unexpected situations, and a clearer hierarchy for finding critical information.

Design opportunity

Organize rich information into meaningful phases and reveal detail progressively, while preserving essential procedural content.

Finding 02 / Complementary media

Different formats.
A fuller understanding.

Video makes subtle techniques and material appearances concrete. Text provides precise parameters, cautions, and optional steps that demonstrations can leave out.

Design opportunity

Coordinate text, visual references, audio, and workflow overviews with the information needed at a particular moment.

Finding 03 / Individual needs

The right detail.
For the person doing the work.

Novices sought explanations, rationales, and precautions. Proficient experimenters focused on core parameters and checkpoints. Preferences also varied with comprehension and recall strategies.

Design opportunity

Offer layered, on-demand information that adapts to expertise and individual cognitive preferences.

Finding 04 / Hands-free access

Less interruption.
Guidance in the workspace.

Participants valued persistent, readable information and interaction that reduced the need to handle paper or touch separate devices while wearing gloves.

Design opportunity

Make guidance accessible through flexible display placement and hands-free interactions that fit laboratory constraints.

Scope & next steps

These qualitative insights come from a small participant sample and one molecular biology workflow. Broader participant groups, diverse laboratory settings, and quantitative and longitudinal evaluations are needed to assess performance, safety, and real-world impact.
